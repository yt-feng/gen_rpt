# Fusion Energy Won't Hit the Grid Before 2040: Strategic Implications for Investors and Policymakers

Despite surging private investment and government backing, nuclear fusion faces a decade-plus path to commercial viability. Here's what decision-makers need to know now.

## Key Takeaways

- Grid-connected fusion electricity will not be commercially available before 2040, based on realistic assessments of major projects like ITER, SPARC, and DEMO. Near-term energy investments should focus on renewables, storage, and fission; fusion remains a.
- First-of-a-kind fusion plants are projected to have levelized costs above $100/MWh through 2040, making them uncompetitive with solar-plus-storage, which is already below $50/MWh and falling. Fusion will require carbon pricing or subsidies to compete.
- Regulatory frameworks for fusion are still being developed and could add 5–10 years to licensing timelines, further delaying commercial operation. Policymakers should begin designing fusion-specific regulations now to avoid future bottlenecks; investors.

## Fusion's Commercial Timeline Extends Well Past 2040

Despite ambitious government roadmaps and startup announcements, the most credible project schedules indicate that fusion will not deliver electricity to the grid before 2040.

The U.S. Department of Energy's Fusion Science & Technology Roadmap, released in 2025, aims to deliver commercial fusion power 'by the mid-2030s.' However, this timeline depends on unprecedented public-private partnerships and assumes rapid progress across multiple technology fronts. The roadmap itself acknowledges that its milestones are contingent on future funding and that it does not commit specific resources.

Looking at concrete projects, the picture is more sobering. ITER, the international tokamak under construction in France, is not expected to achieve first plasma until 2035—a date that has slipped repeatedly. The National Academies' 2021 report on a U.S. fusion pilot plant recommended targeting operation between 2035 and 2040, calling it 'aggressive relative to recent construction of large fusion facilities.' Even the most advanced private projects, such as Commonwealth Fusion Systems' SPARC, aim for first plasma in 2025 and net energy around 2030, but grid connection for a commercial-scale plant would follow years later.

The DOE's Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program, inspired by NASA's approach, has eight awardees working toward pre-conceptual designs by late 2025. While the program has catalyzed over $350 million in private funding against $46 million in federal seed money, it remains an early-stage R&D effort. The path from pre-conceptual design to a licensed, operating power plant typically takes 15–20 years in the nuclear sector.

China's CFETR and the UK's STEP program are also targeting pilot plants in the 2035–2040 timeframe, but both are still in design phases. The historical precedent of fission—which took 30 years from first demonstration to commercial deployment—suggests that fusion's timeline is likely longer than optimists project.

Counter-evidence: Some startups, such as Helion Energy, have announced plans to deliver electricity by 2028. However, these claims lack peer-reviewed validation and are not backed by independent assessments. The gap between announced targets and realistic schedules is a key risk for investors.

Evidence:

- The DOE Fusion Science & Technology Roadmap targets commercial fusion by the mid-2030s but notes it is contingent on future public-private partnerships and funding
- ITER is not expected to achieve first plasma until 2035, according to the ITER Organization
- The National Academies recommends a U.S. fusion pilot plant operational between 2035 and 2040, calling it an aggressive schedule
- DOE Milestone Program awardees are working toward pre-conceptual designs by late 2025, with $350 million in private funding raised since May 2023
- China's CFETR and the UK's STEP program aim for pilot plants in the 2035–2040 timeframe

## Fusion Economics Lag Behind Rapidly Falling Solar-Plus-Storage Costs

Even if fusion becomes technically feasible, its levelized cost of electricity is projected to remain above $100/MWh through 2040, making it uncompetitive with solar-plus-storage, which is already below $50/MWh and declining.

The Fusion Industry Association's 2024 report estimates that first-of-a-kind fusion plants will have LCOE in the range of $100–200/MWh in 2030, declining to $80–150/MWh by 2035 and $50–100/MWh by 2050. In contrast, Lazard's 2023 Levelized Cost of Energy Analysis puts solar-plus-storage at $40/MWh in 2030, falling to $35/MWh by 2035 and $25/MWh by 2050. The gap is substantial and persistent.

Fusion's capital costs are a major driver. A single fusion plant is expected to cost billions of dollars, with construction timelines of 10–15 years. High-temperature superconducting magnets, tritium breeding systems, and plasma-facing components are all expensive and unproven at scale. The DOE's FY 2027 budget request for Fusion Energy Sciences is $755.3 million, a decrease of $50.4 million from FY 2026, indicating that public funding is not growing as fast as needed.

Operational costs are also uncertain. Fusion plants will require regular replacement of plasma-facing components and tritium fuel, which is scarce. The current global supply of tritium is about 5 kg per year, primarily from CANDU reactors, while a single 1 GW fusion plant could consume 56 kg per year. Without effective breeding, tritium supply will be a critical bottleneck.

Counter-evidence: Some fusion advocates argue that costs will fall rapidly with learning curves, similar to solar. However, fusion is a complex, capital-intensive technology with limited opportunities for mass production. The learning rates for nuclear fission have been much lower than for solar, and fusion is likely to follow a similar trajectory.

The implication is that fusion will require significant policy support—carbon pricing, subsidies, or mandates—to compete. In a world where solar-plus-storage is already cheaper, fusion's value proposition rests on its ability to provide baseload power with low land use and no intermittency. But those advantages may not justify the cost premium.

Evidence:

- Fusion Industry Association LCOE estimates: $100–200/MWh in 2030, $80–150/MWh in 2035, $50–100/MWh in 2050
- Lazard solar-plus-storage LCOE: $40/MWh in 2030, $35/MWh in 2035, $25/MWh in 2050
- DOE FY 2027 Fusion Energy Sciences budget request is $755.3 million, down $50.4 million from FY 2026
- Current global tritium supply is ~5 kg/year; a 1 GW fusion plant could require 56 kg/year

## Private Investment Is Booming, but Most Startups Will Fail

Private fusion investment has surged past $9 billion globally, but the vast majority of startups have not yet demonstrated a working prototype, and the failure rate is expected to be high.

According to the Fusion Industry Association, private fusion investment reached over $2.5 billion in 2024 alone, bringing cumulative investment to more than $9 billion. The number of private fusion companies has grown to 45, with the U.S. hosting 25 of them. This influx of capital has been driven by scientific breakthroughs like LLNL's ignition achievement and growing investor interest in deep-tech climate solutions.

However, the milestone data tells a more cautious story. Of the 45 companies surveyed, only a handful have achieved plasma or prototype milestones. Commonwealth Fusion Systems, TAE Technologies, and General Fusion have made notable progress, but many others remain at the concept or design stage. The DOE's Milestone Program, which requires companies to meet specific technical targets, has seen its eight awardees collectively raise $350 million in private funding—but the program is still in its early phase.

The high failure rate is typical for deep-tech ventures. Historical analogs in advanced nuclear and fusion suggest that less than 20% of startups will reach a working prototype, and even fewer will achieve commercial deployment. The risk of capital loss is significant.

Counter-evidence: Some investors argue that the fusion industry is maturing, with increasing government support and a growing talent pool. The FIA reports that fusion companies now employ over 4,000 people, up 34% from 2023. However, employment growth does not guarantee technical success.

For investors, the key is to differentiate between companies with credible technical roadmaps and those with only hype. Due diligence should focus on milestones achieved, peer-reviewed results, and the strength of partnerships with national labs and utilities.

Evidence:

- Private fusion investment reached over $2.5 billion in 2024, cumulative over $9 billion (Fusion Industry Association)
- 45 private fusion companies globally, 25 in the U.S. (FIA 2024 report)
- DOE Milestone Program awardees raised $350 million in private funding since May 2023
- Fusion industry employment exceeds 4,000, up 34% from 2023 (FIA)

## Regulatory Uncertainty Could Add a Decade to Fusion Timelines

Fusion reactors currently lack a dedicated regulatory framework in most countries, and if they are regulated like fission reactors, licensing could take 10–15 years, further delaying commercialization.

In the United States, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is still developing a regulatory framework for fusion. In 2023, the NRC voted to regulate fusion under the same framework as fission, but the specifics are still being drafted. The UK's Environment Agency and the European Commission are also working on fusion-specific regulations, but no final rules have been issued.

The fission licensing process typically takes 10–15 years, including site selection, environmental review, and safety analysis. If fusion is subject to similar requirements, the timeline from design to operation could extend well beyond technical readiness. The DOE's FY 2027 budget request includes funding for an Integrated Blanket and Fuel Cycle Test Facility, but regulatory design is still in early stages.

Counter-evidence: Some advocates argue that fusion is inherently safer than fission—it cannot melt down and produces no long-lived waste—and therefore should face lighter regulation. However, regulators are likely to be cautious with first-of-a-kind technology, especially given public sensitivity to nuclear energy.

The regulatory bottleneck is a non-technical risk that investors and policymakers must address. Early engagement with regulators, development of safety standards, and international harmonization could help reduce delays.

## Strategic Positioning for a Post-2040 Fusion World

While fusion is not imminent, its long-term potential is too large to ignore. Companies and governments should begin preparing now for a future where fusion could reshape energy markets.

Fusion's ultimate impact will depend on its cost trajectory, regulatory environment, and competition from other low-carbon technologies. If fusion can achieve LCOE below $50/MWh by 2050, it could become a major baseload power source, particularly in regions with limited renewable resources or high land costs.

For energy investors, the key is to maintain optionality. This means investing in fusion startups with strong technical teams and clear milestones, but also hedging with investments in renewables, storage, and advanced fission. Corporate strategists should monitor fusion developments and consider partnerships with leading companies.

Policymakers should continue to fund fusion R&D, but also invest in enabling technologies like high-temperature superconductors, tritium breeding, and advanced materials. International collaboration, as with ITER, can share costs and accelerate progress.

Counter-evidence: Some analysts argue that fusion will never be cost-competitive with solar-plus-storage, which is improving faster than expected. If that is the case, fusion's role may be limited to niche applications like industrial heat or hydrogen production.

The bottom line: fusion is a long-term bet with enormous upside but significant risk. The next 15 years will be critical for determining whether fusion can overcome its technical, economic, and regulatory challenges. Decision-makers should prepare for multiple scenarios.

- DOE Fusion Science & Technology Roadmap aims for commercial fusion by mid-2030s
- Private fusion investment exceeds $9 billion globally (FIA)
