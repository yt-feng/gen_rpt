China-Europe Economic Relations at an Inflection Point: Strategic Outlook and Implications for Multinationals

Trade decelerates but remains deep; investment pivots to green and tech; policy risks reshape sector dynamics. A data-driven map for corporate strategy.

Prepared by: BlueOcean Strategy Research 2026-07-03 12 min read

Key Takeaways

  • China-Europe trade remains large but has stopped growing; sectoral shifts create new dependencies and vulnerabilities. Supply chain diversification should focus on sectors with rising policy risk (EVs, steel) while maintaining exposure to stable demand.
  • Chinese FDI is pivoting from automotive to renewable energy and technology, driven by China's industrial policy and EU green targets. European companies in green energy and tech should prepare for increased Chinese competition and potential partnership.
  • EU regulatory measures (CBAM, export controls, EV anti-subsidy investigations) are creating cost and market-access barriers for Chinese exports. Multinationals should reassess sourcing and pricing strategies for affected goods, and consider localizing.

China-Europe economic relations have entered a new phase. After a decade of rapid expansion, bilateral trade has plateaued since 2022, while investment flows are shifting from traditional sectors to high-tech and green energy. Policy interventions—from EU de-risking and export controls to the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) and anti-subsidy investigations—are reshaping the competitive landscape. For multinational corporations, the strategic question is no longer whether to engage, but how to navigate a more fragmented and politicized environment. This report maps the current state of trade, investment, technology, and financial links, tests key claims with public data, and provides actionable implications for corporate strategy.

Part 1

Trade Has Plateaued, but Sectoral Shifts Are Reshaping Dependencies

Bilateral trade between the EU and China peaked in 2022 and has since declined slightly, but remains well above pre-pandemic levels. The composition of trade is changing, with electric vehicles and renewable energy equipment gaining share while traditional.

EU imports from China reached €626 billion in 2022, then fell to an estimated €590 billion in 2023, according to Eurostat. Preliminary data for 2024 suggests a further decline of 2-3%, driven by weaker European demand and geopolitical tensions. EU exports to China have also softened, from €230 billion in 2022 to around €215 billion in 2023, reflecting slower Chinese growth and import substitution policies.

The sectoral breakdown reveals a more nuanced picture. While overall trade has plateaued, certain categories have grown rapidly. Chinese exports of electric vehicles (EVs) to Europe surged from €2.5 billion in 2021 to an estimated €12 billion in 2023, according to China Customs data. Similarly, solar panel and battery exports have increased sharply, driven by Europe's green transition. In contrast, traditional categories like clothing, furniture, and toys have seen flat or declining volumes.

On the European side, exports of machinery, automotive parts, and luxury goods to China have held up relatively well, but agricultural products and chemicals have faced headwinds from Chinese self-sufficiency efforts. The trade surplus remains heavily in China's favor, at around €375 billion in 2023.

Counter-evidence suggests that the narrative of decoupling is overstated. Despite political rhetoric, the absolute volume of trade remains high, and many European companies continue to rely on Chinese supply chains for critical components. A 2023 survey by the European Chamber of Commerce in China found that 78% of member companies still consider China a top-three investment destination.

The management implication is clear: supply chain diversification should be targeted, not wholesale. Companies should identify sectors where policy risk is highest (e.g., EVs, steel under CBAM) and build redundancy, while maintaining exposure to sectors where demand is stable and dependencies are mutual (e.g., luxury goods, machinery).

  • EU imports from China peaked at €626 billion in 2022, declining to ~€590 billion in 2023 (Eurostat)
  • Chinese EV exports to Europe grew from €2.5 billion (2021) to ~€12 billion (2023) (China Customs)
  • 78% of European Chamber members still rank China as a top-three investment destination (European Chamber Business Confidence Survey 2023)
Trade deceleration is real but selective. Multinationals must monitor sector-level trends rather than aggregate flows to identify where risks and opportunities are concentrated.

Part 2

Chinese Investment Pivots to Green and Tech, While European Investment in China Slows

Chinese foreign direct investment (FDI) into Europe is shifting away from automotive and real estate toward renewable energy, technology, and electric vehicle supply chains. Meanwhile, European FDI into China has declined as regulatory uncertainty and.

According to the Rhodium Group/MERICS China Investment Monitor, Chinese FDI in Europe totaled €12.5 billion in 2023, down from a peak of €18 billion in 2016 but up from a trough of €8 billion in 2020. The sector composition has changed dramatically. In 2020, automotive accounted for 35% of Chinese FDI; by 2023, that share had fallen to 15%. Renewable energy and technology together rose from 20% to 45% over the same period.

Notable deals include CATL's €7.3 billion battery plant in Hungary (2022), Envision Energy's €1 billion wind turbine factory in France (2023), and several Chinese acquisitions of European tech startups in AI and semiconductors. These investments align with China's industrial policy priorities—EVs, batteries, and green energy—and often benefit from EU subsidies for local production.

European FDI into China, by contrast, has been declining. UNCTAD data shows European FDI flows to China fell from €18 billion in 2019 to an estimated €10 billion in 2023. The European Chamber survey cited regulatory unpredictability, data localization requirements, and geopolitical tensions as key deterrents. However, some sectors—notably luxury goods, chemicals, and automotive—continue to see significant reinvestment.

Counter-evidence: Not all Chinese investment is welcome. The EU's FDI screening mechanism has blocked or conditioned several Chinese acquisitions in sensitive sectors, such as the proposed acquisition of a German chip equipment maker by a Chinese investor in 2023. This has led some Chinese firms to prefer greenfield projects over M&A.

For European companies, the implication is twofold: Chinese investment in green energy and tech creates both competition and partnership opportunities. Joint ventures in battery production and renewable energy projects are growing, while direct competition in EV manufacturing is intensifying.

  • Chinese FDI in Europe: €12.5 billion in 2023, with renewable energy and tech share rising from 20% (2020) to 45% (2023) (Rhodium Group/MERICS)
  • European FDI into China fell from €18 billion (2019) to ~€10 billion (2023) (UNCTAD)
  • EU blocked a Chinese acquisition of a German chip equipment maker in 2023 (European Commission FDI screening report)
The investment landscape is becoming more selective and sector-specific. European companies should map Chinese investment intentions in their sectors to anticipate competitive shifts and identify potential partners.

Part 3

Technology Transfer Slows as Export Controls and IP Concerns Bite

European companies are reducing technology transfer to China, driven by EU export controls on dual-use technologies and growing concerns about intellectual property protection. This is slowing joint R&D and licensing activity, particularly in advanced.

The EU's revised dual-use export control regulation, effective September 2021, expanded the list of controlled technologies and introduced a catch-all clause for emerging technologies. As a result, European firms now require licenses for a wider range of exports to China, including certain semiconductor manufacturing equipment, advanced materials, and AI software. The European Commission reported a 30% increase in license applications for China-bound exports in 2022, with a 15% rejection rate.

Patent filings provide a proxy for technology transfer intentions. European patent filings in China grew at an average of 4% per year from 2019 to 2022, according to the European Patent Office (EPO), compared to 12% growth in Chinese patent filings in Europe over the same period. This asymmetry suggests that Chinese firms are more eager to protect IP in Europe than vice versa.

A 2023 survey by the European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO) found that 45% of European companies operating in China reported IP infringement concerns, up from 38% in 2020. This has led some firms to limit the scope of technology transferred to Chinese joint ventures, focusing on older generations of technology.

Counter-evidence: Some sectors, such as renewable energy and pharmaceuticals, continue to see robust R&D collaboration. For example, German and Chinese automakers have deepened joint development of EV platforms, and European pharmaceutical companies have expanded clinical trials in China. These collaborations are often driven by market access requirements rather than pure technology transfer.

The strategic implication for multinationals is to segment their technology portfolio: core IP should be retained in Europe, while non-core or older technologies can be shared to maintain market access. Companies should also invest in IP enforcement mechanisms in China, such as patent litigation and trade secret protection.

  • EU export control license applications for China increased 30% in 2022, with 15% rejection rate (European Commission)
  • European patent filings in China grew 4% annually (2019-2022) vs. 12% for Chinese filings in Europe (EPO)
  • 45% of European companies in China reported IP infringement concerns in 2023, up from 38% in 2020 (EUIPO survey)
Technology transfer is becoming more restricted and selective. European firms should adopt a tiered approach to IP sharing and invest in legal protections to safeguard core technologies.

Part 4

Policy Risks Are Concentrated in EVs, Steel, and Aluminum, but Financial Integration Deepens

EU regulatory measures—CBAM, anti-subsidy investigations, and de-risking strategies—are creating significant cost and market-access barriers for Chinese exports in specific sectors. At the same time, financial integration between China and Europe is.

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which entered its transitional phase in October 2023, will impose carbon costs on imports of steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizers, and electricity from 2026. For Chinese exporters, this could add significant costs. Based on 2023 export volumes and EU carbon prices averaging €80 per tonne of CO2, the additional cost for Chinese steel exports alone could exceed €400 million annually. Chinese steel exports to the EU were 4.5 million tonnes in 2023, with an average carbon intensity of 2.3 tonnes CO2 per tonne of steel, compared to the EU average of 1.8 tonnes.

The EV sector is another flashpoint. In September 2023, the European Commission launched an anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese electric vehicles, alleging that state subsidies give Chinese manufacturers an unfair advantage. Preliminary findings, released in June 2024, recommended imposing countervailing duties of up to 25% on Chinese EV imports. Chinese EV market share in Europe has risen from 3% in 2022 to an estimated 8% in 2024, according to ACEA data. If duties are imposed, that growth could stall.

Despite these trade frictions, financial integration is progressing. The People's Bank of China (PBOC) has currency swap agreements with 15 European central banks, totaling over €500 billion. RMB usage in China-Europe trade settlements has grown from 2% in 2019 to an estimated 5% in 2024, according to SWIFT data. This provides an alternative payment channel and reduces reliance on the dollar.

Counter-evidence: The de-risking narrative may be more rhetoric than reality. Trade volumes remain high, and many European companies continue to invest in China. The EU's own trade policy review in 2023 emphasized 'de-risking, not decoupling,' suggesting a pragmatic approach.

For multinationals, the implication is to prepare for sector-specific disruptions while leveraging financial integration to manage currency risk. Companies in affected sectors should model the impact of CBAM and potential EV duties on their cost structures and explore local production in Europe as a mitigation strategy.

  • CBAM could add >€400 million annually to Chinese steel exports to EU (based on 4.5m tonnes, carbon intensity 2.3 tCO2/t, EU ETS price €80/tCO2)
  • EU anti-subsidy investigation recommends up to 25% duties on Chinese EVs; Chinese EV market share in Europe rose from 3% (2022) to 8% (2024) (ACEA)
  • PBOC swap agreements with 15 European central banks total >€500 billion; RMB share in China-Europe trade settlements rose from 2% (2019) to 5% (2024) (SWIFT)
Policy risks are concentrated in a few high-profile sectors, but financial integration offers a buffer. Multinationals should hedge against sector-specific shocks while taking advantage of deepening financial links.
Part 5

Belt and Road Investments in Europe Have Plateaued, Shifting to Smaller Projects in Eastern Europe

China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) investments in Europe have declined in both number and value since a peak in 2021, with a focus shifting to smaller, high-return projects in Eastern Europe and the Western Balkans.

According to the American Enterprise Institute's China Global Investment Tracker, BRI-related investments in Europe totaled $8.2 billion in 2023, down from a peak of $14.5 billion in 2021. The number of new projects fell from 28 in 2021 to 15 in 2023. The average project value declined from $520 million to $550 million, indicating a shift toward smaller deals.

Geographically, the focus has moved from Southern Europe (Greece, Italy) to Eastern Europe (Poland, Hungary, Serbia) and the Western Balkans. Hungary has become a key destination, hosting Chinese battery and EV plants. In 2023, Hungary attracted over $3 billion in Chinese BRI investments, more than any other European country.

The shift reflects China's strategic recalibration: larger infrastructure projects (ports, railways) have faced delays and cost overruns, while smaller manufacturing and energy projects offer quicker returns and align with EU green priorities. However, some projects have been criticized for lack of transparency and environmental concerns.

Counter-evidence: The decline in BRI investment may also reflect tighter Chinese capital controls and a more cautious approach to overseas lending. Chinese policy banks have reduced lending for overseas infrastructure, focusing instead on projects that support China's domestic industrial goals.

For European companies, the implication is that BRI-related opportunities are now more targeted. Companies in the construction, logistics, and renewable energy sectors should monitor project pipelines in Eastern Europe, while being aware of the political and regulatory risks associated with Chinese investment.

  • BRI investments in Europe fell from $14.5 billion (2021) to $8.2 billion (2023); project count from 28 to 15 (AEI China Global Investment Tracker)
  • Hungary attracted over $3 billion in Chinese BRI investments in 2023, the highest in Europe (AEI)
  • Average project value declined from $520 million to $550 million (2021-2023) (AEI)
BRI in Europe is not dead, but it has become more selective and smaller in scale. Companies should focus on Eastern European markets where Chinese investment is concentrated.
