Financial services chart of the week: war-risk premiums surge
The escalating war in Iran has triggered a rapid repricing of geopolitical risk by global insurers, particularly for marine war-risk insurance centred on Gulf shipping routes. Insurers have started reassessing exposure for vessels operating in and around the Strait of Hormuz, a maritime corridor through which roughly one-fifth of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas trade passes. Premiums are surging, and the availability of cover has tightened, as some underwriters limit their appetite for high-risk transits. Insurance remains available typically on a voyage-by-voyage basis, particularly for energy and bulk commodity trades.
The war will not end quickly and is highly likely to last longer than the four to five weeks that Mr Trump originally announced. We believe direct losses for the global insurance industry are likely to remain manageable. War-related losses are typically ring-fenced within specialised war-risk and political-risk policies, and much of this exposure is heavily reinsured and priced for episodic geopolitical losses. However, if hostilities intensify, particularly if attacks directly target shipping or energy infrastructure, insurance markets could tighten further, gradually pushing up premiums, and by extension, freight costs for energy cargo.
We expect war-risk premiums to remain elevated in the near term. Premiums that previously averaged 0.2-0.25% of a vessel’s value have climbed to 1-1.5% in recent days, according to US-based broker Marsh McLennan. Most tankers are valued between US$200m and US$300m. At pre-conflict premium levels of 0.25%, a transit through the Strait of Hormuz would have cost roughly US$500,000-US$750,000 in war-risk insurance, according to market estimates. If premiums approach 1%, the cost per voyage could exceed US$2m. If premiums increase further to 3% of vessel value, which in our view is an extreme but plausible scenario, the insurance cost for a single tanker transit could reach as high as US$7m.
The surge in premiums has coincided with a sharp slowdown in shipping activity. Ship-tracking data indicate that tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen by over 90% from normal levels, and about 400 vessels are anchored outside the waterway rather than attempting to transit. During the crisis five ships on average have transited the strait each day, compared with an average of 100 before the conflict.
Government intervention could stabilise the insurance market, but it will not replace private capacity. Donald Trump, the US president, has indicated that the US could provide naval escorts for tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz and has ordered the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) to provide political risk insurance and financial guarantees for maritime trade in the Gulf. The agency could also provide reinsurance for losses up to US$20bn for hull and cargo exposures.
However, the scale of the backstop is limited. The DFC currently has access to about US$154bn of the US$352bn required to underwrite the 329 oil vessels operating in the region, according to estimates by JPMorgan Chase, a US-based bank. The agency has a statutory liability cap of US$205bn through 2031, of which it had already used up US$51.5bn by end-2025. This means any expansion of support would require congressional approval.
Private insurers remain the primary provider of war-risk cover. On March 11th Chubb, a Swiss insurer, announced that it would act as the lead US insurer under the emerging framework designed to maintain coverage for tankers moving through the region. Meanwhile, Lloyd’s of London, an insurance and reinsurance marketplace, stated that it is engaging with the DFC and other stakeholders to support insurance solutions for vessels transiting the Gulf.
Even if insurance is available, shipping volumes may recover only gradually. Security risks to vessels and crews remain the primary concern for shipowners. As a result, tanker traffic through the strait may remain below normal levels in the near term unless naval escorts or a broader de-escalation reduce operational risks.
