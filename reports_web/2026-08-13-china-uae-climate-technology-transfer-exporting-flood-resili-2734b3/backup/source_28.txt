Since the heavy flooding last April, the UAE has recalibrated and made major changes to its infrastructure and policies. Recognizing the need for future-proof infrastructure, the UAE launched an $8.2 billion drainage project to expand rainwater drainage capacity by 700%. Specialized committees were formed to assess vulnerabilities and redesign systems with weather resilience in mind. Updated building codes and urban planning guidelines are now being drafted to better withstand extreme weather. “This storm was a wake-up call—not just for developers, but for the entire built environment supply chain,” says Grace Gergi of SARA Group , a Specification Manager who works closely with urban planning and construction teams. According to Dr Adam Fenech , climate change expert and Provost at Canadian University Dubai , the UAE’s National Center for Meteorology (NCM) plays a vital role through real-time weather forecasting and early warning systems. With its network of advanced weather stations, Doppler radars, and cloud-seeding aircraft, the NCM supports timely storm response and rainfall enhancement. “In the UAE, extensive natural plains increase the country’s vulnerability to flooding,” says Dr. Mariam Rashed, Assistant Professor of Civil and Environmental Engineering at Khalifa University . “By integrating AI technologies with geospatial data, it becomes possible to accurately identify high-risk areas by analyzing water accumulation patterns and storm forecasts. In May 2024, UK Defra Ministers launched a £91 million fund to address urgent drainage issues and update water management infrastructure after the severe winter storms of 2023/24. The Environment Agency manages this Storm Recovery & Asset Improvement Fund, which focuses on repairing and enhancing systems maintained by Internal Drainage Boards across lowland England. Programme Duration and Benefits The initiative will run until March 2026 and is expected to benefit over 250,000 homes and businesses by improving flood and land drainage assets. Additionally, more than 450,000 hectares of agricultural land are set to gain, with progress already observed on 200,000 hectares. To date, 279 projects across 94 IDBs have received funds, with an allocation of £52.6 million. Funding Tranches and Key Actions The programme operates in distinct stages: - Tranche 1: Immediately addresses storm recovery by repairing pumps, fixing bank slips, removing debris, and mitigating extra energy costs. - Tranches 2A and 2B: Focus on long-term improvements by installing new pumps, integrating telemetry technology, adding solar power capabilities, and implementing nature-based initiatives. Impact and Demand Despite a high level of demand – with funding requests reaching £163 million – the programme has already delivered significant results. Infrastructure improvements include nearly six kilometres of riverbank stabilisation, over 600 bank slip repairs across 125,000 hectares, and enhancements to support fish- and eel-friendly operations through improved pump systems. The acquisition of 32 mobile pumps and the upgrade of 73 pumping stations to remote telemetry systems have notably strengthened flood response and safety measures. This blend of immediate repairs and long-term upgrades not only tackles the damage caused by past storms but also reinforces drainage resilience to better withstand future challenges. The collaboration between Internal Drainage Boards and the Environment Agency is central to the UK’s ongoing efforts in climate adaptation and flood mitigation strategies. Policies to Overcome Floods & Climate Change in Pakistan Pakistan needs strong policies that focus on both prevention and preparedness. These include floodplain zoning to stop construction in vulnerable areas, integrated water resources management with dams, barrages, and rainwater harvesting, and early warning systems for timely evacuation. Reforestation, protection of wetlands, and climate-smart urban planning should also be made mandatory. Furthermore, community-based disaster management, insurance schemes for farmers, and access to international climate funds can strengthen resilience against natural disasters.
