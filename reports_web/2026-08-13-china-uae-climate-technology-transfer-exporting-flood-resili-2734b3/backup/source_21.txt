On 16 April 2024, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) experienced an unprecedented Extreme Rainfall Event (ERE) that produced record-breaking accumulations and widespread flooding, particularly in Dubai. The highest 24-hr rainfall total was recorded at Khatm al-Shakla (Al Ain), with 254.8 mm. Several other stations also recorded exceptionally high totals exceeding 100 mm, including 233.3 mm at Kalba, 219.3 mm at Al Marmoom, and 144 mm at Dubai International Airport. This study investigated the dynamic and thermodynamic drivers of the event using the Multi Source Weighted Ensemble Precipitation (MSWEP) dataset and ERA5 reanalysis. The results indicate that, on 16 April, a deep mid-tropospheric trough and an associated cut off-low generated strong upper-level divergence and large-scale ascent over the UAE. At the same time, substantially enhanced moisture transport from the Arabian Sea (associated with to strong moisture advection) and the Red Sea (supported by the Red Sea Trough) increased precipitable water and intensified moisture convergence across the region. Positive sea surface temperature anomalies over the surrounding seas may have further supported lower-tropospheric moisture availability. A well-defined 700 hPa cyclonic circulation on 16 April further supported mesoscale convective organization, while moist static energy profiles indicated a highly energetic troposphere conducive to deep convection. In addition, the extremity of the event is consistent with springtime teleconnections associated with El Niño decay conditions, which may have contributed to a large-scale background environment favorable for above-normal April rainfall over the region, with composite anomalies of approximately 30%. These findings highlight the critical role of coupled dynamic and thermodynamic processes in generating high-impact rainfall extremes. Improving the understanding of these processes is essential for advancing predictive modeling capabilities and strengthening climate-resilient planning across the region, in alignment with the goals of the “Early Warning for All” initiative.
Extreme rainfall events (EREs) have increased in frequency and severity in recent decades, raising concerns about climate resilience and disaster preparedness 1 . Recent analyses indicate that inreasing precipitation variability, together with atmospheric warming, raises the likelihood of record-shattering rainfall through a combination of thermodynamic moistening and circulation changes 2 . Ongoing warming and shifts in large-scale circulation are also altering the probability and severity of local extreme events 3 . High-impact rainstorms frequently cause flash floods, infrastructure damage, and socio-economic disruption, underscoring the need to advance process-based understanding of their drivers 4 , 5 . EREs are among the most consequential weather hazards globally 6 , 7 .
A recent global assessment shows that cities in warm–humid climates are particularly prone to enhanced urban precipitation relative to those in cold–dry climates, with larger populations, stronger urban heat islands, and higher aerosol loads associated with measurable increases in rainfall 8 . Scientific evidence further indicates that urbanization can shape long-term precipitation statistics and influence the occurrence of extreme weather 9 , 10 . Urban development may therefore act as a plausible local-scale modifier by interacting with the storm environment and potentially influencing rainfall distribution, runoff generation, and flood consequences 11 .
Thermodynamically, a warmer climate increases the atmosphere’s water-holding capacity through the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship, thereby generally intensifying heavy-precipitation extremes across many regions. Heavy rainfall extremes are intensifying at rates broadly consistent with moisture-driven scaling for accumulation periods ranging from hours to days 12 , 13 .
Recent studies over the Arabian Peninsula show that extreme convective rainfall is frequently produced by Mesoscale Convective Systems (MCSs) that develop when synoptic scale forcing coincides with anomalous moisture supply from the adjacent Oceans 14 , 15 . A canonical configuration for heavy rainfall includes warm, moist low-level inflow from the Arabian Sea, Arabian Gulf, and Red Sea, a mid-level trough that destabilizes the column, and a southward-displaced subtropical jet that enhances upper-level divergence 16 . The intersection of upper-level cold air with abundant boundary-layer moisture and complex topography increases static instability and orographic ascent, initiating deep convection that organizes into long-lived, rain-efficient MCSs 17 . Nelli 18 characterized the regional atmospheric circulation associated with major weather events over the UAE–Empty Quarter region and highlighted the importance of large-scale circulation, boundary-layer processes, and organized convective activity, with deep convective clouds occurring more frequently during March–April. Case-study and climatological analyses indicate that springtime extremes over the southern Arabian Peninsula commonly feature enhanced 925–700 hPa moisture advection from adjacent warm seas, focused low-level convergence, and jet-/trough-related ascent, providing a favorable backdrop for MCS genesis and maintenance 16 . High-resolution simulations of the March 2016 UAE “mega-storm” explicitly resolve this pathway, linking anomalously warm marine moisture sources and concentrated low-level inflow to flooding 19 . Recent convection-permitting simulations over the UAE’s complex terrain further demonstrate how moist onshore flow can interact with orography to produce realistic MCS sequences when moisture supply is elevated 20 .
The Arabian Sea has warmed markedly since the early 1990s, with a pronounced acceleration during spring from approximately 0.07 °C decade −1 to about 0.13 °C decade −1 . This warming enhance evaporation and increase column-integrated water vapour available to the eastern Arabian Peninsula 21 , 22 . During El Niño, moisture transports from both the Red Sea and the Arabian Sea toward the Arabian Gulf tends to strengthen, providing a large-scale modulation that can further elevate springtime rainfall risk 21 . Previous studies have examined (i) the acceleration and seasonality of Arabian Sea warming, including the pre-monsoon “mini warm pool” and persistent positive SST anomalies across the Arabian Sea and adjacent basins 23 ; (ii) the synoptic and moisture controls over the UAE and the broader southern Arabian Peninsula; and (iii) UAE case studies, including the 2016 event and convection-permitting simulations 19 , 20 .
On 16 April 2024, the UAE recorded its heaviest rainfall in at least 75 years, according to the National Center of Meteorology (NCM). A persistent convective system produced torrential rainfall, thunderstorms, and strong winds, leading to widespread flash flooding and severe disruption to transportation. The NCM reported a 24-h rainfall total of 254.8 mm at Khatm Al Shakla, Al Ain. Kalba and Al Marmoom recorded 233.3 mm and 219.3 mm, respectively, while many other locations exceeded 100 mm. These totals are remarkable given that the UAE’s national mean annual rainfall is approximately 100 mm. Dubai International Airport received approximately 144 mm, equivalent to about two years’ worth of rainfall, in a single day, and peak gusts reached 113.4 km h −1 at Tal Al Sarab on 16 April (UAE Annual Climate Assessment Report 2024).
