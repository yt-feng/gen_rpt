Highways, roads, and drainage systems are integral components of a single infrastructure system and must be designed and executed in coordination with each other. Any occurrence of waterlogging, flooding, drainage overflow, or premature pothole formation is generally an indication of inadequacy in the design, drainage provisions, construction quality, or execution. A properly engineered road system, supported by adequately designed and constructed drainage infrastructure, should effectively manage stormwater and prevent water accumulation on the carriageway under the design rainfall conditions. The objective of accurate design is not merely to construct the road, but to ensure its functionality, safety, durability, and performance throughout its intended service life. Therefore, recurring flooding, water accumulation, or deterioration of roads should not be treated merely as maintenance issues; they warrant an assessment of the underlying design parameters, drainage capacity, gradients, levels, hydraulic provisions, and quality of construction.

Sharjah's Dh500 Million Midline Drainage Project Nears Completion to Strengthen Flood Protection Construction of Sharjah's Dh500 million Midline Drainage Project has reached around 70% completion, marking significant progress on one of the emirate's largest infrastructure initiatives. Led by the Sharjah Department of Public Works (SDPW) under the directives of His Highness Sheikh Dr. Sultan bin Mohammed Al Qasimi, the project is designed to improve the drainage of rainwater and groundwater, helping protect communities from flooding while enhancing the resilience of Sharjah's infrastructure. The project features an integrated engineering system spanning 16 kilometres, with approximately 15 kilometres constructed using advanced micro-tunnelling technology at depths of up to 20 metres. It also includes two major pumping stations at Al Ghubayba and Al Sour, enabling water to be efficiently channelled towards the sea. Once fully operational, the network will initially serve 22 key areas, with future phases expanding coverage to 42 districts, including major roads such as King Faisal Street, King Abdulaziz Street, and the Al Wahda extension. Officials say the Midline Drainage Project is a long-term investment in climate resilience and sustainable urban development. By reducing water accumulation, maintaining traffic flow during heavy rainfall, and protecting residential and commercial areas, the project will significantly strengthen Sharjah's ability to manage extreme weather while supporting the emirate's continued growth. Dubai just brought a $22 billion sewer tunnel project to market as a PPP, which is a strange thing to spend that much structuring effort on until you look at why. A PPP means the government isn't funding this out of its own budget directly, rather private investors and lenders put up most of the capital, build and usually operate the asset, and get repaid over a long concession, often 20 to 30 years, either through user fees or fixed payments from the government regardless of usage. 10-years ago this would have just been paid for off the state balance sheet, but now it's running through the same capital structure we'd use for a toll road, such as senior lenders, equity sponsors, a formal concession agreement. Abu Dhabi is doing the same thing at a much bigger scale, around $54 billion of infrastructure being procured through PPPs. Sewage infrastructure getting this treatment is actually the more telling data point than the big flagship projects, because it means the model has moved past the obvious use cases and is now the default approach even for the unglamorous stuff.
