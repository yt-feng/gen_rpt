The long-term structural integrity of reservoir-dam systems is essential for ensuring water security, energy supply, and public safety. Underwater structural defects, driven by complex hydraulic and environmental conditions, pose growing threats to the safe operation of large dams. In recent decades, significant progress has been made in underwater inspection, defect detection, and repair technologies, offering viable alternatives to traditional drawdown-based maintenance. This review systematically synthesizes advances in underwater detection and rehabilitation technologies for reservoir-dam systems, focusing on inspection platforms, positioning and navigation methods, defect identification techniques, and underwater repair materials. It critically examines key challenges, including limited transferability of marine-derived technologies, environmental interference, cumulative operational errors, and real-world applicability. This paper further categorizes existing technologies by maturity and proposes problem-oriented research directions to support the development of intelligent, reliable, and field-deployable systems for underwater defect management in large dams.
Global population growth, rapid urbanization, and sustained socio-economic development have substantially increased water and electricity demands. Concurrently, global efforts to address climate change and enhance disaster resilience have created new opportunities for hydropower expansion 1 . According to the International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD) 2 , the total number of large dams worldwide reached 62,339 by 2024. China leads globally in the number of high dams and reservoirs with 683 dams exceeding 70 m in height, including 244 above 100 m and 23 surpassing 200 m 3 . However, environmental changes, extreme operating conditions, seismic activity, and geological hazards have resulted in increasing structure aging and performance degradation 4 , 5 , 6 . Consequently, numerous reservoir dams face potential safety risks and structural deficiencies 7 . If left unaddressed, these conditions can lead to catastrophic failures such as dam breaches, uncontrolled flooding, and significant disruptions to water and energy supply, resulting in immense economic losses, environmental damage, and potential loss of life. The escalating risks underscore the urgent need for advanced, reliable, and cost-effective underwater inspection and repair technologies to ensure the long-term safety and operational continuity of these critical infrastructures. The complicated operational environments of these systems create significant challenges for underwater inspection, repair, and safety diagnostics. While some defects can be addressed through underwater interventions and routine maintenance, others necessitate costly dewatering procedures. Dam structures require interdisciplinary approaches to underwater inspection and repair due to their diversity, varying depths, complex geometries, and heterogeneous defect profiles. In recent years, underwater inspection and reinforcement technologies have emerged as critical priorities in hydraulic engineering 8 , 9 , 10 . These challenges underscore the necessity for advances in precise detection techniques and rapid safety diagnostic methods for underwater defects in reservoir–dam systems essential for ensuring long-term dam safety 11 , 12 . Critically, effective underwater defect rehabilitation directly supports key UN Sustainable Development Goals by ensuring water security through contamination prevention and reliable supply (SDG 6), fostering resilient infrastructure innovation for renewable hydropower (SDG 9), protecting urban communities from catastrophic failures (SDG 11), and enabling climate adaptation/mitigation via flood control and carbon-neutral energy (SDG 13) 13 .
Given the rapid advancements in this interdisciplinary field, a comprehensive review that synthesizes current strategies, assesses their strengths and limitations, and identifies critical challenges is both timely and essential to guide future research and development. This paper presents a comprehensive review of underwater detection, repair, and retrofitting technologies for reservoir–dam systems.
Incidents threatening reservoir-dam safety are a global concern. Data from the US, China, and Europe highlight that defects occur across dams of all types and ages 14 (Fig. 1 ). The high cost of rehabilitation underscores the need for proactive maintenance 15 . Unlike marine environments, reservoir-dam systems present unique challenges: narrow valleys affecting GPS signals, extreme hydrostatic pressures at high dams ( > 100 m), and complex underwater currents near intakes. These factors necessitate specialized equipment distinct from standard oceanographic tools. Traditionally, underwater inspections and repairs have involved draining reservoir and conducting manual inspections to identify structural defects 16 . While this approach was standard practice, it presents significant disadvantages: it is time-consuming, wastes water, and poses risks of environmental pollution and ecological harm 17 , 18 . Numerous existing high dams, particularly those in the 300 m class, lack emergency drawdown facilities or required conditions for drawdown 19 , resulting in limited safety assurance capabilities.

Despite the clear and pressing need for underwater inspection and repair technologies in reservoir-dam systems, the actual implementation of such operations remains fraught with scientific and engineering difficulties. The complexity of the underwater environment, coupled with the diversity of structural forms and defect types, imposes significant constraints on the performance of existing detection and intervention systems. To guide future technological development and field deployment, it is essential to systematically analyze the key challenges that currently limit the effectiveness, reliability, and safety of underwater operations 20 , 21 . The following section outlines four major categories of challenges, ranging from theoretical model transferability to the propagation of cumulative operational errors, which collectively hinder the advancement of underwater defect management in dam engineering.
Development of underwater operational technologies and equipment initially emerged from the marine engineering sector 22 , primarily supporting ocean observation, offshore oil and gas extraction, deep-sea mineral mining, marine scientific research, and military applications 23 , 24 . These activities involve substantial technical requirements, investment, risks, and returns. Gradually, the application scope has expanded to shipbuilding 25 , inland navigation 26 , water conservancy and hydropower 7 , and nuclear power 27 , advancing toward lightweight, modular, and intelligent systems. However, the operational conditions for underwater inspection, repair, and reinforcement in reservoir-dam systems differ significantly from marine environment (See detail in Fig. 2 ). Technologies developed for marine purposes cannot be directly transferred. Notable distinctions include: (1) Reservoir valleys generally feature substantially narrower surface areas compared to open oceans. Large river valleys typically span several hundred meters, while mountainous gorges may measure only tens of meters, affecting underwater inspection equipment positioning accuracy 28 , 29 , 30 . (2) Reservoir acoustical environments are complicated by U- shaped or V-shaped valleys, complex hydraulic structures, uneven reservoir bottoms, and siltation 31 . These factors significantly affect acoustic wave reflection, diminishing the effectiveness 32 , 33 . (3) Unlike the high-visibility of marine waters, reservoirs typically contain turbid waters with poor visibility. Moreover, bed-load and suspended sediments increase noise and signal attenuation in both acoustic and optical detection systems 16 , 20 , 21 . In addition, high-flow velocity zones near hydraulic structures threaten detection equipment safety 34 .

The aquatic environment is further impacted by unsteady and multiphase flows, which modify medium parameters and attenuate detection signals. Underwater acoustic-optical imaging face challenges from external noise, clutter interference, and internal system or quantization noise, creating complex perturbations across acoustic, optical, and hydrodynamic fields. This results in significant signal-to-noise ratio fluctuations; in some cases, signals become completely obscured by noise, degrading imaging quality and rendering targets undetectable 35 , 36 . Most underwater defects in hydraulic structures initiate or manifest at the surface before progressing inward. These features often concealed by biofouling are typically subtle and unstructured, leading to significant uncertainty in defect characterization 37 . Moreover, under the influence of various natural and anthropogenic influences, dam service behavior exhibits characteristics such as multi-source uncertainty, nonlinearity, and temporal variability 38 , 39 . The evolution mechanisms and failure modes of underwater structural defects are intricate. Current research inadequately addresses correlations between potential hazards and lacks clarity regarding topological relationships among underwater defects. This is exacerbated by insufficient deep-water data fusion methods and continued reliance on single-method diagnostics 40 , 41 resulting in ambiguity in structural performance assessment(Fig. 3 ). Moreover, complex flow regimes, poor water quality, and intricate structural configurations within reservoir-dam systems present significant challenges for underwater inspection, repair, and reinforcement operations. Manual operations are often constrained by diver endurance and expertise 42 . Divers encounter risks from water pressure, strong currents, and low temperatures 43 , making safety, efficiency, and consistency difficult to maintain. Meanwhile, underwater vehicle operations face limitations in perception, precision, and autonomous decision-making capabilities 44 , 45 . These constraints impede robotic task refinement, and current level of automation and intelligence remains insufficient to replace human involvement in repair and reinforcement tasks 46 , 47 . The complexity of the reservoir-dam environment amplifies operational uncertainty and difficulty, making it impossible to fully address hidden defects. Therefore, hazard detection and interpretation remain significant challenges. Research and development specifically adapted to reservoir environmental conditions and boundary constraints are urgently needed. Prior objectives should include enhancing detection speed and accuracy, reducing computational and communication costs, and simplifying model complexity.

Various types of errors can significantly impact on underwater inspection, repair, and reinforcement operations, affecting their accuracy and effectiveness. These errors primarily manifest in four categories, as depicted in Fig. 4 for details.
Physical entity geometric errors: Complex external environments and structural behavior of the reservoir–dam system can lead to structural deformation in the physical geometry of hydraulic structures during long-term operation. Such deformation creates discrepancies between actual dimensions and measured results 48 , 49 , deviating from original design and construction data. Consequently, relying exclusively on construction drawings for underwater operations fails to meet precision requirements. Additionally, the inherent accuracy limitations of inspection equipment contribute to geometric measurement inaccuracies 50 , 51 .
Interactive timing errors during operational process mapping: Timing errors refer to data distortions resulting from desynchronization during data acquisition, processing, and transmission. These errors may arise from imprecise device clocks, signal transmission delays, or inconsistent data processing speeds 52 . Inadequate clock synchronization between different devices—or between underwater units and shore-based systems—combined with network latency and signal transmission uncertainties, can also introduce errors during data exchange 53 . In complex underwater operations requiring multiple devices working in coordination, timing errors can disrupt task sequencing 54 , 55 , compromising the continuity and effectiveness of the overall workflow.
Mutual feedback errors between underwater operational equipment and defects: Due to the variability of underwater terrain and structural complexity, mutual feedback errors mainly appear as positioning errors 56 , 57 . Positioning systems in underwater equipment, including GPS, long/short/ultra-short baseline systems, and inertial navigation systems, face limitation from complex hydraulic structures, uneven reservoir bottom topography, and siltation 58 . During multi-equipment, multi-scenario, and multi-mission underwater inspection and repair operations, cumulative error effects 59 may result in relative positioning deviations between sequential operational points.
Underwater environmental effect errors: The distinctive characteristics of underwater environments, such as light scattering and absorption, acoustic wave propagation characteristics 60 , and variations in temperature and pressure, result in uneven propagation of acoustic and optical waves used by different imaging methods 61 . This causes energy scattering, phase shifts, and reflection and diffraction across different media, ultimately reducing accuracy and reliability of detection data 16 . Furthermore, the complex and dynamic nature of underwater environments can adversely affect operational equipment and repair materials, increasing operational difficulty and uncertainty, thereby reducing the precision of underwater operations.

These four types of errors can be further categorized into systematic, measurement, positioning, environmental, human, and random errors. Systematic, measurement, and positioning errors typically have greater impact on outcomes and should be controlled through the usage of high-precision equipment, accurate calibration, and optimized workflows. While random and environmental errors remain unavoidable, they can be mitigated through repeated measurements, data filtering, and other techniques. Human errors require minimization through standardized procedures and strict operational protocols.
Although substantial advances have been achieved in underwater detection, positioning, and repair technologies for reservoir–dam systems, translating these developments into reliable large-scale engineering practice remains challenging 3 , 62 .
