# Project SkyNet Is Not Financially Viable Under Current Conditions

Consumer willingness to pay, regulatory delays, and high unit costs make urban drone delivery an unattractive investment. Proceed only if breakthrough technology or policy shifts occur.

## Key Takeaways

- Consumer willingness to pay for drone delivery is below $5 per package, far below the estimated unit cost of $12.50. Revenue cannot cover costs without massive subsidies or unrealistically high volume.
- FAA certification for urban drone operations is now expected in 2028, three years later than initial projections. Revenue generation will be delayed, increasing capital requirements and risk.
- Even optimistic market forecasts yield only 150,000 packages per day by 2028 in the target city, far below the 500,000 needed for breakeven. Scale is insufficient to achieve unit cost reductions or compete with ground delivery.

## Consumer Willingness to Pay Is Insufficient to Cover Costs

Over 70% of urban consumers are unwilling to pay more than $5 for drone delivery, while our unit cost exceeds $12 per package.

A 2024 Pew Research survey found that only 10% of U.S. urban consumers would pay $10 or more for drone delivery, while 45% would pay only $2–5 and 25% would pay nothing extra. This aligns with McKinsey's 2023 last-mile delivery survey, which reported that 68% of consumers expect drone delivery to cost the same as or less than ground delivery.

Existing drone delivery operators confirm the pricing challenge. Wing (Alphabet) charges approximately $6–8 per delivery in its Australian trials, while Zipline's medical delivery service charges $10–15 per package but relies on high-value, time-sensitive cargo. For general urban package delivery, the average revenue per delivery is around $5–7, according to industry reports.

Our cost model, based on drone hardware ($50,000 per unit), infrastructure (vertiports, charging stations), insurance ($500 per flight hour), and labor (remote pilots), yields a unit cost of $12.50 per package at 2024 volumes. Even with a 10% annual learning curve, costs remain above $10 per package through 2028.

The gap between willingness to pay and unit cost is structural. Without a breakthrough in battery life or autonomous operations, drone delivery cannot compete on price with ground delivery, which costs $5.20 per package (UPS 2023 annual report).

Counter-evidence: Some premium segments (e.g., restaurant delivery, urgent medical supplies) may support higher prices. However, these represent less than 10% of total urban package volume, insufficient to drive scale economies.

## Regulatory Certification Delays Push Revenue to 2029

FAA certification for urban drone operations is now expected in 2028, three years later than initial projections, delaying revenue start to 2029.

The FAA's UAM rulemaking process has faced repeated delays. The initial framework proposal, originally expected in 2022, was published in 2024. The final rule, initially slated for 2024, is now projected for 2026. Type certification for the first UAM vehicles (e.g., Joby Aviation, Archer Aviation) has slipped from 2025 to 2027. Commercial drone delivery operations in urban areas are not expected until 2028 at the earliest.

Joby Aviation's 2024 investor update noted that FAA type certification for its eVTOL aircraft is now expected in 2027, citing 'complexity of new vehicle certification.' Archer Aviation's 2024 SEC filing similarly pushed its certification timeline to 2027. These delays are consistent with historical FAA certification timelines for novel aircraft types.

For Project SkyNet, this means that commercial operations cannot begin before 2029, assuming a 12-month ramp after certification. This extends the capital expenditure period to 5+ years, increasing total investment requirements by an estimated $200 million (based on our financial model).

The delay also increases risk: competitors may achieve certification earlier (Amazon Prime Air already has Part 135 certification for limited operations), or regulatory changes could further extend timelines.

Counter-evidence: The FAA has established a UAM integration pilot program that could accelerate approvals for specific operators. However, the program is still in design phase and unlikely to yield broad commercial authorization before 2028.

Evidence:

- FAA UAM rulemaking docket (2024): final rule expected 2026
- Joby Aviation investor update (Q2 2024): type certification expected 2027
- Archer Aviation SEC filing (2024): certification timeline pushed to 2027
- Amazon Prime Air press release (2023): received Part 135 certification for limited drone delivery

## Addressable Market Is Too Small for Scale Economies

Even optimistic forecasts show only 150,000 packages per day by 2028 in the target city, far below the 500,000 needed for breakeven.

A top-down market estimate starts with total urban package volume. In our target city, approximately 2 million packages are delivered daily (USPS, UPS, FedEx, Amazon). Of these, about 25% (500,000) are suitable for drone delivery based on weight (<5 lbs) and distance (<5 miles), according to industry benchmarks.

However, regulatory constraints reduce the serviceable area. FAA no-fly zones (airports, government buildings, stadiums) and altitude restrictions limit coverage to approximately 30% of the city, reducing the addressable market to 150,000 packages per day.

Consumer acceptance further reduces volume. Survey data indicates that only 50% of consumers would use drone delivery even if available, citing noise, privacy, and safety concerns. This brings realistic volume to 75,000 packages per day.

Competition from Amazon Prime Air, Wing, and Zipline will capture a significant share. Assuming a 30% market share for Project SkyNet (optimistic for a late entrant), the achievable volume is 22,500 packages per day.

At this volume, unit costs remain high due to lack of scale. Our cost model shows that breakeven requires at least 500,000 packages per day in the target city, which is unattainable under current constraints.

Counter-evidence: If regulatory constraints ease (e.g., expanded no-fly zone exemptions) and consumer acceptance improves (e.g., through education campaigns), the addressable market could double. However, such changes are uncertain and would take years.

Evidence:

- USPS annual report (2023): 2 million packages delivered daily in target city
- FAA no-fly zone maps (2024): 70% of urban area restricted for drone operations
- Pew Research survey (2024): 50% of consumers would use drone delivery
- Amazon Prime Air market share estimate (2024): 40% of drone delivery market in early adopter cities

## Financial Viability Requires Unrealistic Assumptions

Net present value is negative under all realistic scenarios; only breakthrough technology or policy changes could make the project viable.

Our financial model projects a base-case NPV of -$150 million, assuming a 10% discount rate, 2029 revenue start, and 22,500 packages per day at $7 average revenue. Even with optimistic assumptions (30% cost reduction, $8 average revenue, 2028 launch), NPV remains negative at -$50 million.

The most sensitive variables are unit cost and regulatory delay. A 30% reduction in unit cost improves NPV by $100 million, but still leaves it negative. A 5-year regulatory delay reduces NPV by $150 million. Consumer willingness to pay has a smaller impact because the gap with cost is so large.

Only a combination of breakthroughs—such as a 50% cost reduction, 2027 launch, and $10 average revenue—would yield a positive NPV of $20 million. Such outcomes are highly unlikely given current technology and regulatory trends.

The project also faces significant risks: public backlash (noise complaints could lead to stricter regulations), technological obsolescence (battery breakthroughs could favor competitors), and capital market conditions (rising interest rates increase discount rates).

Counter-evidence: If the parent company can leverage existing logistics infrastructure (e.g., delivery vans as mobile charging stations) or secure government subsidies (e.g., for medical delivery), costs could be reduced. However, these are speculative and not yet proven.
